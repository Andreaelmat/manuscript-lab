# Chapter 1. The Flood

The flood reached the mile road on Friday, 2 May, and took the lower fences before noon.

Mara stood at the last dry rise with her shoes in her hand. The village was a brown line behind her. She had promised Lev she would walk as far as the coast, and she did not look back until the water covered the road.

# Chapter 2. The Scar

Lev was waiting under the mile-oak, older than the tree looked and no kinder.

The scar on Mara's left cheek was white in the cold. He did not ask where she had earned it. She did not offer the story. They walked until the oak was only a post behind them.

# Chapter 3. Nineteen

"You are still nineteen," Lev said. "Do not let the ferrymen add years for the fare."

Mara was nineteen. She had been nineteen since the January snow, and the flood had not given her another birthday. Lev carried the bread. She carried nothing she would need to explain.

# Chapter 4. The Church Step

They passed the church because the road did. The door was shut. A blue cup sat on the step where someone had left water for the walkers and then forgotten it.

She left the blue cup on the church step and did not pick it up again. Lev watched her do it and said nothing, which was his way of agreeing.

# Chapter 5. No Tomas Left

At the ditch he spoke of his brother for the first and, he said, the last time.

"Tomas drowned in the January ice," Lev said. "There is no Tomas left to write."

Mara nodded. The name did not belong to anyone living on their side of the flood. She repeated it once, so she would not have to ask it later, and then she let it go.

# Chapter 6. What They Carried

By evening the village was gone from the road. They had a loaf, a knife, and the clothes they wore.

Nothing from the church step came with them. Mara said so because Lev liked inventories, and he answered, "Then we are as poor as we meant to be."

# Chapter 7. The Dry Well

Years before the flood, in August, the well behind the church had gone dry. Mara remembered the bucket coming up with dust, and the priest laughing as if weather were a joke he had already heard.

That August was not this May. The road under her shoes was wet, and the well she remembered belonged to a different year.

# Chapter 8. The Letter

A boy on a mule brought a letter to the ditch camp and would not say who paid him.

Lev broke the seal with his thumb. The last line was large enough for Mara to read over his wrist: "I am at the port. Do not tell the road my name. — Tomas"

"Wrong mouth," Lev said, and fed the edge of the paper to the cook-fire until the signature curled. Mara did not yet know which Tomas the fire had eaten.

# Chapter 9. The Cloak

The river was the first thing on the third day that refused them.

Osip the ferryman looked at their shoes and put a grey cloak over Mara's shoulders. "It keeps the spray off," he said. "Bring it back if you come back. If you do not, hang it somewhere honest."

She closed it at the throat. The wool smelled of tar and other people's crossings.

# Chapter 10. The Ferry Bargain

"The boat is full," Osip said.

"Then I will stand in the water until it is not," Mara said.

He looked at the book on the thwart, then at the flood-line on her ankles. "There is room on the floor. It ships water. Write your name in the book before we push off, and the floor is yours."

Mara set the cloak's wet hem on the book and waited. She wanted the far bank. He wanted the name. Neither of them named the price again.

Osip poled them over and stayed with the boat. "I do not cross twice for the same flood," he said. "Do not look for me on the road."

# Chapter 11. Nineteen Days

The walk from the oak to the coast took nineteen days. No birthday fell inside those days.

The month was still May when they counted the evenings. Mara did not become older by walking, and Lev did not claim that she had.

# Chapter 12. What Mara Had Not Been Told

On the ninth day a carter going the other way spoke to Lev at the ditch and lowered his voice.

No one had told Mara that the bridge had fallen. She heard the wheels, not the words. When she asked, Lev said the man had been talking about horses. She believed him because she had no other account.

# Chapter 13. Still the Ferryman's Wool

Rain found them again on the twelfth day. Mara pulled the grey cloak over her head and kept walking.

They had not seen the ferryman since the river. Osip's boat was a story they told to stay awake, not a person on the road. The cloak was the only thing he had given that could still be touched.

# Chapter 14. The Boy Who Signed

Lev stopped where the road bent toward the port smell and gave the burned letter its meaning back in speech.

"The letter was from my son," he said. "He is Tomas too. He signed his dead uncle's name because the soldiers open letters from children, and they do not open letters from the drowned."

The brother stayed in the January ice. The boy at the port was alive, afraid, and badly named. The signature and the death could both stand.

# Chapter 15. The Fire

They dried their clothes at a brick kiln that still had one mouth burning.

Mara turned her right cheek to the fire so the scar would warm. The heat found the line of it at once. She did not say that the scar had moved.

# Chapter 16. Two Things She Should Not Have Had

The port road had a pump and a tin shelf. Mara drank from the blue cup she had carried since the church, and the water tasted of brick.

"Osip told me yesterday that the bridge had fallen," she said.

Lev looked at her over the pump handle. He did not correct her. No ferryman had walked with them, and nobody had given her the bridge before this sentence.

# Chapter 17. How the Flood Was Counted

The cranes were in sight when the leaving was told again, not as Lev's guess but as the chapter's own account.

The flood had come on Thursday, 2 May, and the road had vanished before noon. The sentence was repeated by the narration, with the same weekday, and nothing later took it back.

# Chapter 18. The Age at the Coast

On the nineteenth evening Mara hung the grey cloak on a nail by a hostel door. The wool was still Osip's, and the nail was as honest as the road had allowed.

At the coast Mara was twenty-six, the age she had been when she left the village. The nineteen days were done. No birthday had come. The number of her years had changed without the book showing the change.
