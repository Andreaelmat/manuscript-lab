# Dialogue and style

Use this file for dialogue, narrative voice, repetition, exposition, and prose rhythm. Respect a consistent authorial choice. Do not normalize a voice because another voice would be clearer.

## Dialogue

Judge a spoken exchange by what the speakers are doing to each other.

- They want something from the exchange, or they are avoiding something.
- Their diction, sentence length, and evasions are not interchangeable, when the book has more than one speaking person.
- The exchange ends with a shift in information, power, or attention.
- Neither person explains a fact both of them already know, unless the performance of explaining is itself the point.

"The dialogue is unrealistic" is not a finding. Quote the turn that could be said by either person, or the turn that exists only to brief the reader, and say what dramatic job it misses.

Subtext is not mandatory. Some people in some books say what they mean. Do not file directness as a defect. File explanation that repeats what the action already showed.

## Voice

The narrative voice can be formal, fragmented, repetitive, dialectal, or plain. Judge breaks in that contract, not distance from a neutral style.

A register break is evidence when the narration suddenly uses a vocabulary the rest of the book does not use, and the shift is not a character's thought or a marked quotation. One unusual word is not a break. A paragraph that sounds like a different narrator is.

Do not correct grammar inside dialogue unless the book has established that this speaker's grammar is not the point and the error looks accidental. When you are unsure, call it interpretive and say so.

## Repetition

Distinguish three cases:

- Motif: the repetition returns with variation, irony, or increased pressure. Leave it, or record it as a strength.
- Orientation: the first statement tells the reader something they need. Later brief echoes may be doing memory work. Be slow to file these.
- Restatement: a fact, image, or explanation returns without new pressure, information, or voice. This is the usual repetition finding, and it is interpretive.

Quote the first use and the later use. Say what the later use fails to add. Do not quote a single word and call it a pattern unless you can show the repeated passage.

Repetition inside one person's speech can be character. Say so before you call it waste.

## Exposition

Exposition is a problem when it stops a scene to deliver facts the scene does not use, or when dialogue carries a briefing. It is not a problem when the narrator's way of telling is the book's method, or when a character discovers the fact under pressure.

Prefer "this explanation arrives before it has a consequence" to "show, don't tell." Telling is a legal instrument.

## Rhythm

Comment on rhythm only where it changes the reader's attention: a climax told in the same cadence as the travel before it, a revelation buried in a list, a scene of decision summarized after a page of furniture. Quote a short span and name the effect. Do not propose alternative sentences.

## Over-explanation

File over-explanation when the narration glosses a line or gesture that was already clear. Quote the line and the gloss. If the gloss changes the meaning, it is not redundant. Say what it adds, and do not file it.

## Interventions

Name the cut, the merge, or the missing pressure. Do not rewrite the author's line. Do not recommend synonyms. Do not flatten dialect, sentence fragments, or long sentences that are consistent with the voice.

## Priority

Use `medium` when restatement or undifferentiated speech dulls a scene the book is relying on. Use `low` for a local echo. Use `high` only when the voice break or the briefing makes a central relationship or revelation hard to believe. Most style findings are not high.
